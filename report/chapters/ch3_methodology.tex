\chapter{Retrieval Methodologies}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Classical Foundations}
\label{sec:classical}

The agentic components described later do not replace classical retrieval; they
schedule it. Every sub-query the Planner emits is ultimately answered by one of
the three mechanisms below, so their individual blind spots determine what the
agent layer has to compensate for.

\subsection{Sparse Retrieval: BM25}
\label{sec:bm25}

BM25~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic} scores a document $d$ against a query
$q$ by summing, over the query terms, a saturating function of term frequency
weighted by inverse document frequency:
\[
  \mathrm{score}(q,d) \;=\; \sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{IDF}(t)\cdot
  \frac{f(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}
       {f(t,d) + k_1\!\left(1 - b + b\,\dfrac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)} .
\]
The saturation parameter $k_1$ controls how quickly repeated occurrences stop
adding score; the length normalisation parameter $b$ controls how strongly long
documents are penalised. The values used here, $k_1 = 0.9$ and $b = 0.4$
(\texttt{retrieval.sparse}), sit below the classical $1.2$ and $0.75$
deliberately: the indexed unit is a single Wikipedia lead paragraph averaging
4.05 sentences and 531.9 characters, not a full document. At that length
repetition carries little extra evidence and length differences are largely
incidental, so heavy normalisation would penalise informative paragraphs for
being informative.

BM25 matches rare surface forms exactly, needs no training, is interpretable
term by term, and answers in milliseconds. It also supplies the analysed
lexicon and per-term document frequencies that the routing rules of
Section~\ref{sec:tools} consume, which nothing else in the system can provide.
Its weakness is vocabulary mismatch: a query and a relevant passage sharing no
surface term after stemming score zero against one another, however obviously
they concern the same thing.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval}
\label{sec:dense}

A dense retriever embeds queries and passages independently into a shared
vector space and ranks by similarity. This work uses
\texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}~\cite{xiao2023cpack}, a 384-dimensional bi-encoder,
with vectors L2-normalised so that inner product equals cosine similarity, and
an exact FAISS flat inner-product index. Exact rather than approximate search
is affordable because the corpora are small --- 66{,}581 and 54{,}957 passages
--- and it matters because any recall the system fails to achieve is then
attributable to the retrieval method rather than to the data structure. The
model is used asymmetrically, as its authors intend: passages are encoded bare,
queries are prefixed with \texttt{"Represent this sentence for searching
relevant passages: "}.

Its strength is paraphrase, exactly the case BM25 cannot see; its weakness is
the mirror image. Query and passage never meet, and each is compressed into 384
dimensions before comparison, a bottleneck that preserves topic far better than
identity --- so two similarly named people or two films sharing a title are
liable to be conflated. The two failure modes are close to complementary, which
is the standing argument for combining them.

\subsection{Hybrid Fusion and Reranking}
\label{sec:hybrid}

The two ranked lists are combined by Reciprocal Rank Fusion
(RRF)~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal}, which discards the scores and combines
positions:
\[
  \mathrm{RRF}(d) \;=\; \sum_{r \in R} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)},
  \qquad k = 60 ,
\]
with $k$ from \texttt{retrieval.hybrid.rrf\_k}. Rank-based fusion is preferred
over score normalisation because BM25 scores are unbounded and depend on corpus
statistics and query length, while cosine similarities from a normalised
encoder occupy a narrow band near the top of $[-1,1]$: the two share no scale
and no principled conversion. Normalisation manufactures one, but the fused
score then depends on the shape of the candidate list, so a single outlier at
the tail can reorder the head. Ranks are ordinal and scale-free, and the
ordinal information is the part on which the two retrievers can meaningfully be
said to agree.

Fusion improves recall but leaves head precision largely to chance, which is
what reranking addresses. A cross-encoder --- \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2}
here --- concatenates query and candidate and runs full self-attention over the
pair, so their terms interact directly. It is more expressive than a bi-encoder
for the same reason it is more expensive: nothing about the passage can be
precomputed, so scoring $n$ candidates costs $n$ forward passes and admits no
index. That cost fixes the depth parameters, \texttt{top\_n} $=50$ before
reranking and \texttt{top\_k} $=10$ after. Reranking is also often decorative,
so when the fused top-1 already leads the runner-up by a relative RRF margin of
at least \texttt{rerank\_margin\_gate} $=0.15$ the fifty forward passes cannot
change the head of the ranking and are skipped --- a saving counted in the
trace rather than assumed.

\section{Why Single-Shot Retrieval Fails on Multi-Hop Needs}
\label{sec:singleshot}

Everything in Section~\ref{sec:classical}, up to and including the
cross-encoder, computes a scoring function $s(q, d)$. The three methods differ
enormously in expressive power --- from a bag-of-words sum, through a
384-dimensional dot product, to full cross-attention over the pair --- but they
do not differ in their arguments. All of them assume that the relevance of a
document is determinable from the query and that document. For a large class of
information needs this assumption is correct and unremarkable. For a bridge
question it is false, and the consequence is not that these systems perform
poorly but that they are solving the wrong problem.

\paragraph{A worked example.} Consider question
\texttt{5a7793275542992a6e59df04}, drawn from the HotpotQA evaluation sample of
Section~\ref{sec:sampling}:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Which country is the firm that owns Babycham located?}
\end{quote}

\noindent
The gold answer is \emph{Australia}, and the two gold supporting facts are the
opening sentences of two passages:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Babycham}, sentence 0 --- ``Babycham is the trade name of a light,
sparkling perry invented by Francis Showering, a brewer in Shepton Mallet in
Somerset, England; the name is now owned by Accolade Wines.''

\textbf{Accolade Wines}, sentence 0 --- ``Accolade Wines is a major global wine
business with headquarters in South Australia.''
\end{quote}

The first hop is easy, and easy in a way that flatters BM25 specifically:
``Babycham'' is a near-unique term in a 66{,}581-passage corpus, so lexical
matching on that term alone puts the correct passage at rank one. This is
precisely the situation routing rule R4 of Section~\ref{sec:tools} exists to
detect --- though, as that section records, in the evaluated runs the
Planner's own tool hint settled every routing before R4 could be consulted.

The second hop is not merely harder. Compare the query terms ---
\emph{country}, \emph{firm}, \emph{owns}, \emph{Babycham}, \emph{located} ---
against the second gold sentence. Not one of them occurs in it. BM25 has
nothing to match and assigns no score on any discriminative term. A bi-encoder
produces a non-zero similarity, but the query embedding is dominated by
\emph{Babycham} and a possessive relation, while the passage embedding says
\emph{wine business}, \emph{global}, \emph{South Australia}. The passage sits
among the corpus's other company profiles, and nothing in it distinguishes it
from any of them with respect to this query.

\paragraph{Why the reranker does not close the gap.} There are two reasons, and
only the second is fundamental. The first is mechanical: a cross-encoder
reorders a pool it is handed, so a passage that never enters the pool cannot be
recovered by reordering, and widening the pool postpones the problem rather
than solving it.

The second reason survives any pool width and any model capacity. Suppose the
\emph{Accolade Wines} passage is placed in the pool by fiat, and suppose the
cross-encoder is arbitrarily large and perfectly trained. It is presented with
the pair (question, passage) and asked to judge relevance. The information that
would justify a high score --- that Accolade Wines is the firm that owns
Babycham --- is present in neither element of that pair. It is asserted in a
third document. The relevance of the second-hop passage $d_2$ therefore does
not factorise as a function of the query--document pair; it is a conditional
quantity $\mathrm{rel}(q, d_2 \mid d_1)$ depending on a document the ranker was
never given. Increasing model capacity changes the function class being
searched, not the arguments the function receives, so a better ranker is a
better approximation of the wrong quantity. This is why the failure is properly
described as a specification error rather than a ranking error, and it is the
single fact from which the rest of this chapter follows.

\paragraph{What would work.} The query that retrieves \emph{Accolade Wines}
trivially is ``Where is Accolade Wines located?'' --- a query with the same
rare-key property that made the first hop easy. It cannot be written in
advance, because the string ``Accolade Wines'' appears nowhere in the user's
question; it exists only as the output of the first retrieval. The missing
capability is therefore not ranking quality but the ability to issue a second
query whose text is conditioned on the result of the first. That is a control
structure, not a scoring function, and no single-shot architecture can express
it however good its scorer is.

\paragraph{Scope of the argument.} This failure mode is not an edge case.
Bridge questions are 5{,}918 of HotpotQA's 7{,}405 validation questions, or
79.9\%, and 2Wiki's compositional, inference and bridge-comparison types ---
9{,}536 of 12{,}576 questions --- share the same conditional structure.
Comparison questions fail differently, and the distinction is worth stating
rather than blurring: there both gold passages are individually retrievable
from the original query, since both entities are named in it, so the difficulty
is coverage at a fixed cut-off plus the fact that the answer is the output of
an \emph{operation} over two passages rather than the content of either. That
is a weaker claim, and Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} accordingly gives the
two shapes different plan topologies. How far the single-shot baselines fall
below the achievable ceiling is a measurement, reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}; the point here is that the ceiling exists by
construction and cannot be lifted by tuning.

\section{Agentic Retrieval}
\label{sec:agentic}

The remedy indicated above is a retrieval process that carries state between
rounds. That is the common thread through the agentic IR literature: treating a
language model as an autonomous agent that plans, invokes tools and
revises~\cite{zhang2024aiagent,wu2025agentic}, whether the domain is scientific
literature~\cite{lala2023paperqa}, multimodal
collections~\cite{schneider2025collex}, telecommunications
networks~\cite{zhang2025toward} or geospatial
workflows~\cite{lee2025multiagent}. The four agents below cover, between them,
the five tasks of the assignment brief's Table~1.

\subsection{Query Decomposition and Refinement}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The Planner converts a question into a directed acyclic graph of at most five
sub-queries. Each node carries its own text, an explicit dependency list, an
intent and an expected answer type, and may contain template placeholders of
the form \texttt{\{\{q1.answer\}\}}, \texttt{\{\{q1.entity\}\}} or
\texttt{\{\{q1.title\}\}}. Execution proceeds in level-synchronous topological
order, and a node's placeholders are substituted from the state of its
dependencies immediately before it is routed and retrieved. The second-hop
query is therefore a genuinely different string at retrieval time from the one
the Planner emitted --- exactly the capability Section~\ref{sec:singleshot}
identifies as missing --- and because the resolved text differs lexically from
the template, routing is recomputed after substitution rather than inherited.

This is the same territory as Self-Ask~\cite{press2023selfask}, which
interleaves follow-up questions inside a single generation, and
IRCoT~\cite{trivedi2023interleaving}, which interleaves retrieval with
chain-of-thought steps. Four differences matter. The plan here is an explicit,
validated data structure rather than a decoding trajectory: cycles, dangling
dependencies, over-long plans and unbound placeholders are repaired
mechanically before execution and every repair is recorded, so a
model-generated plan is never executed on trust. It is a graph rather than a
chain, so a comparison question yields two independent roots retrieved in the
same level where a linear follow-up mechanism must serialise them. Query
expansion rides inside the same planner response as a per-node list of at most
two rewrites, fused with RRF at retrieval time, so expansion costs \emph{zero}
additional model calls. And the plan can be revised on evidence supplied by a
downstream agent (Section~\ref{sec:verification}), which no single-pass
decoding trajectory can do.

Two Planner decisions followed from measurement. The plan's \texttt{strategy}
label is derived from the shape of the validated graph and overwrites whatever
the model reported: on eight representative questions at temperature 0 with a
constrained schema, \texttt{qwen3:8b} produced structurally correct
decompositions 8 times out of 8 but labelled the strategy \texttt{single\_hop}
in 7 of those 8, including unambiguous bridge and comparison cases. It builds
the graph well and names it badly, and since that label gates a deterministic
comparison shortcut, trusting it would silently disable one; both labels are
logged, so their disagreement rate is itself a reportable result about the
self-knowledge of a small local model. Separately, the model reliably emits a
final \emph{combiner} node restating the question and depending on every other
node, whose answer comes from composing its dependencies rather than from any
passage; detecting and skipping it removes a third of all retrieval on a
three-node comparison plan.

The Planner degrades along a deterministic ladder rather than failing: a
comparison template, then a two-node bridge template, then an identity plan
routed to hybrid search with reranking. It is tempting to describe that floor
as the \texttt{hybrid\_rerank} baseline, and an earlier draft of this report
did; the description is wrong, and the ablation grid of
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} is what shows it to be wrong. An identity plan
still passes through synthesis and verification, so the system with the
Planner removed is a one-node agentic system that answers, cites, and can
still re-plan --- not a ranker. What the ladder guarantees is narrower and
still worth having: retrieval itself can never be worse than a single hybrid
query with reranking, so a retrieval deficit against that baseline is a
judgement about what the Planner chose to ask, never an infrastructure failure.

\subsection{Tool-Augmented Retrieval}
\label{sec:tools}

Tools are declared in a registry as a name, a callable, an argument schema and
a natural-language description. Seven are exposed across two families:
\texttt{bm25\_search}, \texttt{dense\_search}, \texttt{hybrid\_search} and
\texttt{rerank} over the indexes, and \texttt{kg\_link}, \texttt{kg\_neighbors}
and \texttt{kg\_path} over the entity graph. The same description strings that
document a tool to a human are the strings injected into the tool-selection
prompt, so model-facing documentation and executable registry cannot drift
apart --- a small property that removes an entire class of silent bug in which
an agent is told about a tool that no longer behaves as described.

Selection among the retrieval tools is a seven-rule decision table over
features of the resolved sub-query: token count after stopword removal,
presence of a quoted span, number and token share of capitalised runs,
out-of-vocabulary rate against the BM25 lexicon, minimum document frequency
among content terms, and whether the node has dependencies. The rules encode
Section~\ref{sec:classical} directly. A planner hint is honoured (R1); an
out-of-vocabulary rate above 0.34 routes to dense search (R2), because BM25
cannot score terms absent from its lexicon; short quoted or proper-noun-dense
queries (R3) and queries containing a term of document frequency three or fewer
(R4) route to BM25, a very rare term being a near-unique key --- the Babycham
case above; dependent nodes (R5) and long or entity-free queries (R6) route to
hybrid, since a bridge-filled query mixes one precise entity with paraphrased
relation text and neither channel alone suffices; and a default row (R7) makes
the table total.

One fact about the table has to be stated before its cost is argued for,
because it changes what the argument is about. Rule R1 honours a tool hint the
Planner may attach to a sub-query, and the decision-table is evaluated first
match wins. In the headline HotpotQA run the Planner attached a hint to every
one of the 854 sub-queries it emitted, so R1 fired on all 575 routing
decisions the trace records and rules R2--R7 never executed once. All seven
rules are implemented and unit-tested; on this data six of them are dead code.
That is not a defect in the rules but a finding about the division of labour
between the two agents: the schema-constrained planning call, which the system
pays for anyway, already carries a per-node tool choice, and the lexical
features the remaining rules consult were never needed to decide anything. The
cost argument below therefore describes a one-rule router in practice, and the
saving it claims is the saving of not asking the model a second time for a
decision it made in its first call. Whether the model's hints agree with what
the lexical rules would have chosen is a measurable question and is recorded
as an open one in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}.

The table costs zero model calls, and that deserves stating as an argument
rather than as an optimisation. An agent that spends a language-model call on a
decision a rule already settles is spending the user's latency budget, not
demonstrating intelligence. At the measured 52 tokens per second a
schema-constrained call costs roughly 3.75\,s, whereas the routing rule is a
handful of look-ups against an already-loaded lexicon; multiplied across
sub-queries, 250 questions, two datasets and nine configurations, that is hours
of wall clock purchased for a decision the default row would often have made
identically. Autonomy is a property of which decisions a system makes without a
human, not of which decisions it routes through a transformer.

\begin{sloppypar}
The claim is nonetheless empirical, and the experiment that would test it was
designed but has not been run. The design was to clear the retriever's
\texttt{heuristic\_shortcut} flag, which routes every sub-query through a
tool-selection prompt instead of the table, run that configuration on the
disjoint 50-question calibration slice, and report the rule--model agreement
rate, the change in nDCG@10 and the change in wall clock. No such run exists
under \texttt{results/}, so no agreement rate is reported anywhere in this
document. There is also a reason the experiment cannot be run as the code
stands: the retriever consults the planner hint before it consults the flag,
so a sub-query carrying a hint is routed by R1 whether or not the shortcut is
enabled, and with a hint on every sub-query the selection prompt is
unreachable. Making the model router reachable requires suppressing the
Planner's hints as well, which is a different experiment from the one designed
and one that would test the lexical rules R2--R7 rather than the router the
system actually used. The paragraph above is therefore a design argument, not
a measured result, and Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} says so again where the
cost figures are reported.
\end{sloppypar}

This is also where reinforcement learning would enter, and why it does not
enter here. Tool routing is a policy over a small discrete action space with an
observable reward, which is textbook territory for a contextual bandit or a
policy-gradient learner. The obstacle is specific and measurable: the reward
would have to derive from gold supporting facts, and the only data carrying
those labels is the evaluation data, so an on-policy learner would be training
on the test set and any gain would be uninterpretable. The one leakage-free
signal available is the 50-question calibration slice, far too small to fit a
policy that reliably beats a seven-rule table. Reinforcement learning is
therefore listed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} rather than
claimed here.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph Traversal}
\label{sec:kg-traversal}

The KG Navigator operates on the entity graph built in
Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}. Entity linking is an alias-trie longest match
over normalised titles and costs no model call. Traversal is bounded at two
hops and twenty-five neighbours per node (\texttt{agents.kg.max\_hops},
\texttt{max\_neighbors}) and expanded in deterministic order of descending edge
weight, so a repeated run explores the graph in exactly the same sequence.

The highest-value operation is bidirectional breadth-first search: given two or
more linked seeds it expands from each and reports the meeting node as the
\emph{bridge entity}. Applied to the example of Section~\ref{sec:singleshot},
the alias ``Accolade Wines'' occurring in the text of the \emph{Babycham}
passage is an edge in the graph by construction, so the bridge entity that the
retrieval stack could not surface by ranking is recovered by a graph walk in
milliseconds, and recovered without a generation step where an equivalent model
call would cost seconds. This is the concrete sense in which graph traversal is
not decorative: it is a second, structurally different mechanism for the one
operation single-shot retrieval provably cannot perform.

Traversal is also the most explainable component, because the path \emph{is}
the explanation. Every edge stores the document identifier, the sentence
identifier and the sentence asserting it, so a traversal result enters the
evidence pool at the same sentence granularity as a retrieved passage, is cited
in the same way, and is scored by the same supporting-fact metrics; a graph
result that could not be cited would be unfalsifiable. The limits should be
equally clear: the graph is induced from mention co-occurrence, not curated
from an ontology, and 23.2\% of HotpotQA edges and 21.0\% of 2Wiki edges carry
no label beyond \texttt{mentions}, so relation semantics are weak and the
structure is best understood as a navigation aid rather than a knowledge base.
Comparison questions are handled accordingly: with two seeds and no path
expected, each seed's attribute-bearing sentences are returned instead of
forcing a spurious connection, and an empty result is a legal outcome.

\subsection{Self-Reflective Validation}
\label{sec:verification}

The Verifier receives the Synthesizer's answer with the evidence pool and
decides whether it is supported. Cited identifiers that do not exist are
recorded as hallucinated citations and contribute no support, which alone
catches a common failure of small local models. A natural language inference
model, \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base}, is then run with each cited
sentence as premise and the answer sentence as hypothesis, and the maximum
probability of entailment becomes the NLI support; the Synthesizer emits that
answer sentence as a separate field precisely so the hypothesis is a
well-formed declarative sentence, which is what an NLI model is trained on,
rather than the usual concatenation of question and answer. Two further signals
are computed --- citation grounding, the fraction of claims with at least one
valid supporting citation, and retrieval agreement, the fraction of executed
sub-queries whose top-ranked title appears among the cited evidence --- and the
four combine as
\[
  \mathrm{conf} = 0.45\,\mathrm{nli} + 0.25\,\mathrm{citation}
                + 0.15\,\mathrm{retrieval} + 0.15\,\mathrm{llm},
\]
with weights from \texttt{agents.verifier.weights}, renormalised when the model
term is absent.

That last term is gated: the adjudication call is issued only when the
confidence computed without it falls inside $[0.40, 0.70]$, the threshold of
0.55 plus or minus an uncertainty band of 0.15, because confident acceptances
and confident rejections cannot have their verdict changed by it. Above the
threshold the verdict is \texttt{accept}. Below it, with re-plans remaining,
the verdict is \texttt{revise} and the Verifier emits a re-plan directive
carrying what could not be established, which sub-queries returned nothing
useful, which entities are already covered, and --- the field that makes the
loop work at all --- the normalised texts of every sub-query already tried.
Without that ban list, an 8B model told only that its previous attempt was
insufficient reliably re-emits the same decomposition and burns both re-plans
for nothing. Below the threshold with no re-plans left the verdict is
\texttt{abstain}, a label rather than a refusal: the best candidate is still
returned, so answer metrics remain computable.

A verifier can be wrong in two directions and the design has to survive both.
Too strict, it triggers re-planning that costs latency and risks replacing a
good answer with a worse one; too lenient, it rubber-stamps errors while
reporting high confidence. Answer selection at termination is therefore the
argument maximising confidence over \emph{all} cycles, ties broken toward the
earlier and cheaper answer, so a bad re-plan cannot silently overwrite a good
first attempt; and the error taxonomy of Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}
contains explicit \texttt{verifier\_false\_accept} and
\texttt{verifier\_false\_reject} categories, computed post hoc against gold
supporting facts, so the loop is falsifiable rather than merely asserted. The
threshold of 0.55 was set a priori. The design called for it to be swept on
the disjoint calibration slice of Section~\ref{sec:sampling} and frozen before
the evaluation run, because it controls the re-plan rate and tuning it on the
evaluation set would corrupt one of the headline agent metrics; what was
actually done is a post-hoc sweep over that slice, reading confidences that a
single run at 0.55 had already produced, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}
reports both what that sweep found and why it is not the experiment the
design specified.

\section{The Role of the Language Model}
\label{sec:llm-role}

The language model is the component that generalises, and everything else in
this system exists to keep the number of calls small and their inputs short.
Three decisions genuinely require it: mapping an unseen compound question onto
a dependency structure, which is open-ended and cannot be enumerated by rules;
composing a short answer span from heterogeneous evidence sentences, which
requires reading; and adjudicating the narrow band where the numeric confidence
signals disagree, which requires judgement arithmetic does not supply.
Everything else is deterministic --- tool routing, entity linking, graph
traversal, entailment, evidence deduplication and ranking, comparison
arithmetic over parsed dates and numbers, and query expansion, which rides
inside the planner call. Each is a decision over a small closed action space
with a cheap and reliable feature, where a model call would add latency and a
new failure mode, malformed output, without adding accuracy. The worst case
over a whole question is therefore 15 calls against a cap of 20, with 4--7
typical, and two are reserved so synthesis and verification always run even
when the Planner has exhausted the budget.

Serving that model locally shaped the design more than any other single factor,
and Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} gives the full configuration. Two
consequences belong here because they changed the method rather than the
deployment. Throughput of 52 tokens per second makes prompt length a latency
cost, which is why the re-plan prompt passes retrieved \emph{titles} rather
than retrieved text, holding it to a few hundred tokens instead of several
thousand. And \texttt{qwen3} is a hybrid reasoning model that emits a
\texttt{<think>} block by default, whose measured effect is worse than added
latency: with thinking enabled and a 128-token prediction budget, the model
spent the \emph{entire} budget on a 503-character reasoning block and produced
no answer at all. Thinking does not merely delay the response, it starves it,
so suppression is applied three ways and whatever leaks through is logged.
Structured output is parsed down a five-rung ladder ending in a repair prompt,
and parse failures are counted per agent --- because if the Planner's rate is
high, what the results measure is partly the fallback ladder rather than the
model, and the report has to say so.

\section{Architectural Summary}
\label{sec:arch-summary}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the resulting system. Read left to right it
is a pipeline: a Planner decomposes, a Retrieval Agent routes each sub-query to
the indexes, a KG Navigator recovers bridge entities from the entity graph, a
Synthesizer composes a cited answer, and a Verifier scores it. The edge that
makes it an agent rather than a pipeline is the dashed one drawn above: below a
confidence of 0.55 the Verifier returns a re-plan directive to the Planner
instead of returning an answer, and the system tries again with an explicit
record of what it already knows and what it must not repeat. Every other
component in this chapter is standard IR machinery arranged carefully; that
backward edge is the contribution, and Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} isolates
its effect by ablation rather than assuming it.

Mapped onto the assignment brief's Table~1, the Planner covers autonomous query
refinement and multi-step retrieval planning, the tool registry and routing
table cover tool-augmented retrieval, the KG Navigator covers knowledge graph
traversal, and the Verifier covers self-reflective validation and fact
checking; Table~\ref{tab:task-mapping} in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} gives
the mapping row by row with the sections that specify and measure each. Five
tasks are addressed by four agents because refinement and planning are the
same act performed at different points in the loop: once before evidence
exists, and again after the Verifier has said what is missing.
