\chapter{Introduction to Agentic AI in Information Retrieval}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{From Reactive Generation to Agency}

A generative language model is a function from a prompt to a completion. Given
an input it emits text and stops. It holds no goal that outlives the call, it
cannot observe what its output caused, and it has no mechanism for concluding
that what it produced was inadequate. That is an accurate description of a
component, and it is also an accurate description of most systems currently
labelled retrieval-augmented: a query is embedded, a ranked list is fetched, the
list is concatenated into a prompt, and a completion is emitted. Retrieve once,
generate once. The behaviour is reactive because nothing in the architecture
allows it to be anything else.

Agentic artificial intelligence names the class of systems built to remove that
limitation. An agentic system pursues a goal across multiple steps, selects its
own actions, invokes external instruments to carry them out, observes what those
instruments returned, and revises its behaviour in light of the
observation~\cite{zhang2024aiagent,wu2025agentic}. Four properties are
load-bearing, and it is worth separating them because systems are routinely
called agentic on the strength of the first three alone.

\textbf{Autonomy} means that the system, not the user, decides what happens
next. The user supplies a goal; every intermediate decision --- which
sub-question to ask, which index to consult, whether the current answer is good
enough to return --- belongs to the system. \textbf{Goal-directed planning}
means the system constructs an explicit representation of the work before doing
it. A plan is an artefact rather than an emergent side effect: it can be
inspected, validated, repaired, compared against a previous plan, and discarded.
\textbf{Tool integration} means the model is not the answer source but the
instrument selector. Facts come from retrieval indexes, graph traversals, and
arithmetic; the model's contribution is deciding which instrument fits which
sub-goal and how the outputs compose. \textbf{Adaptive reasoning} means the plan
is revisable after execution has begun, because an observation can invalidate
it.

The fourth property is the difficult one, and it is the one most often absent.
Autonomy, planning, and tool use can all be present in a system that is
nevertheless a fixed pipeline whose stages happen to be language-model calls:
work flows forward, each stage runs exactly once, and nothing discovered late
can change anything decided early. The industry touchstones the assignment brief
cites --- AutoGPT and Microsoft's Jarvis --- are notable precisely for
attempting the fourth property, letting a model re-enter its own planning stage
on the basis of what its tools returned. The research literature shows the same
trajectory. PaperQA retrieves across full-text scientific articles, judges the
relevance of what it found, and synthesises an answer from the surviving
evidence~\cite{lala2023paperqa}. The Agentic Reasoning framework adds tool-using
sub-agents and a mind-map agent that maintains a structured record of
established relations~\cite{wu2025agentic}. CollEX supports curiosity-driven
multimodal exploration of scientific collections~\cite{schneider2025collex},
agentic contextual retrieval in telecommunications combines multi-source
retrieval with self-reflective validation for network
decision-making~\cite{zhang2025toward}, and multi-agent geospatial copilots
decompose remote-sensing workflows across specialised
agents~\cite{lee2025multiagent}. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} examines these
approaches in detail. What matters here is the shape they share and the question
they leave open: whether the feedback edge, once actually implemented, earns the
compute it consumes.

\section{The Limits of Static Ranking}

Classical information retrieval is the study of a scoring function. Given a
query and a corpus, rank the documents by estimated relevance and return the
top $k$. The probabilistic relevance framework and its BM25 instantiation
formalise this for lexical matching~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}; dense
bi-encoders replace term overlap with proximity in a learned embedding
space~\cite{xiao2023cpack}; reciprocal rank fusion combines rankings from
several such systems without requiring their scores to be
commensurable~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal}; and heterogeneous benchmarks such as
BEIR measure how well any of it transfers to unseen
domains~\cite{thakur2021beir}. These are genuine and cumulative advances. Every
one of them improves the \emph{ordering of a single list produced from a single
query}.

Consider a question from the HotpotQA development set~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa},
which this report reuses as a running example:

\begin{quote}
\textit{When did the tour begin for the album Taylor Swift released on
October 22, 2012?}
\end{quote}

\noindent The corpus contains the two passages that settle it. The passage
titled \texttt{Red (Taylor Swift album)} states that \textit{Red} is the fourth
studio album by Taylor Swift and was released on October 22, 2012. The passage
titled \texttt{The Red Tour} states that the tour was launched in support of
that album and began on March 13, 2013 in Omaha, Nebraska. The gold answer is
\textit{March 13, 2013}.

Of the 66{,}581 passages in the processed HotpotQA corpus, exactly zero contain
both \textit{October 22, 2012} and \textit{March 13, 2013}. This is not a
statement about ranking quality. It is a statement about the corpus: there is no
document for a ranker to place first, because no single document answers the
question. A single-shot retriever is structurally incapable here, and so is
every refinement layered on top of it. A cross-encoder reranker permutes a
candidate set; it cannot insert into that set a passage whose relevance depends
on a fact the query does not contain.

The lexical structure of the example sharpens the point. The bridge entity ---
the album title \textit{Red}, the one term connecting the question to the
answer-bearing page --- does not occur in the question at all. It has to be
\emph{derived} before it can be searched for. A lexical retriever cannot match a
term that was never typed, and a dense retriever embeds the question as a whole,
so the sub-need ``identify the album'' is diluted by the sub-need ``find its
tour date'' rather than issued as a query in its own right. The information need
is a composition of two atomic needs, and single-shot retrieval resolves
compositions only in the fortunate case where some passage already states the
composition. This is the compositionality gap that Press et al.\ measure
directly~\cite{press2023selfask}, and it is what motivates interleaving
retrieval with reasoning steps rather than separating
them~\cite{trivedi2023interleaving}.

Scale turns the difficulty into an impossibility. The two corpora used in this
work hold 66{,}581 and 54{,}957 passages; the number of ordered passage
\emph{pairs} is on the order of $10^9$ in each case. Materialising the
compositions in advance is not an option, so the composition must be inferred
from the question, which is a reasoning operation rather than a ranking one. Two
further pressures compound it. The HotpotQA distractor setting populates each
question's candidate pool with passages deliberately chosen to be topically
adjacent to the gold ones, so surface similarity is actively adversarial. And
relevance for the first hop is defined only relative to an intermediate goal the
user never stated: \texttt{Red (Taylor Swift album)} is relevant not because it
resembles the question but because it supplies the term the second query needs.
No relevance judgement over the original query alone can express that.

\section{Problem Statement}

The problem this project addresses is the design of an information retrieval
agent that, given a natural-language question and a static corpus, does four
things a ranker does not. It must \emph{interpret the intent} behind a question
whose surface form under-specifies the retrieval work required. It must
\emph{decompose} that intent into an ordered set of sub-goals with explicit
dependencies, so that the result of one retrieval can be substituted into the
query of the next. It must \emph{orchestrate} the resulting multi-step workflow,
choosing an appropriate retrieval instrument for each sub-goal and, where the
corpus admits a structured view, traversing that structure rather than searching
it. And it must \emph{judge its own answer} against the evidence it actually
retrieved, before returning anything.

The fourth requirement is where a pipeline and an agent part company, and it is
the central claim this report exists to test. The system described here is, with
one exception, a forward pipeline: plan, execute, aggregate, synthesise. The
exception is a single backward edge. A Verifier scores each candidate answer
against the evidence cited in support of it, blending natural-language-inference
entailment, citation grounding, and the agreement between retrieved passages and
the answer into one confidence value. When that confidence falls below the
configured threshold, the Verifier does not answer. It emits a
\texttt{ReplanDirective} back to the Planner carrying what could not be
established, which sub-queries returned nothing useful, and --- the field that
makes the loop converge rather than spin --- the normalised text of every
sub-query already attempted, which the next plan is forbidden to repeat. One
edge is a modest architectural change and a categorical behavioural one: it is
the only mechanism in the system by which a late observation can revise an early
decision.

The system operates under a deliberate constraint. It runs entirely on local
hardware: inference is served by \texttt{qwen3:8b} at Q4\_K\_M quantisation
through Ollama on a single RTX 5060 with 8~GB of VRAM, measured at 52 tokens per
second warm, and no component holds an API key. The constraint sharpens the
research question rather than weakening it. At that throughput a
schema-constrained model call costs several seconds of wall clock, so every call
must be justified against a deterministic alternative. The resulting design
treats ``where should an agent reason?'' as a budgeting problem: tool selection
is decided by a seven-rule decision table over cheap lexical features and costs
zero model calls, entity linking is alias matching, graph traversal is
bidirectional breadth-first search, and the confidence blend is arithmetic. The
worst case for a full agentic question is 15 model calls against a hard cap of
20, and the typical cost is four to seven. A system that cannot afford to think
everywhere is forced to be explicit about where thinking pays --- and, as
Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} reports, the division of labour it settles on
is not always the one that was designed: in the headline run the Planner
attached a tool hint to every sub-query, so the first rule of the routing table
decided every routing and the other six were never reached.

\section{Objectives and Contributions}

The objective is a working agentic retrieval system that covers all five tasks
of Table~1 of the assignment brief and whose central architectural claim is
tested rather than asserted. The contributions are four.

\textbf{First, a four-agent architecture with full task coverage.} A Planner
performs autonomous query refinement and multi-step retrieval planning, emitting
a validated directed acyclic graph of sub-queries linked by inter-node
placeholders. A Retrieval Agent performs tool-augmented retrieval, selecting
among BM25, dense, and hybrid search under a conditional reranking gate. A
Knowledge Graph Navigator performs graph traversal over an entity graph built
from corpus text alone, using bidirectional search to surface the bridge entity
that multi-hop questions turn on. A Verifier performs self-reflective validation
and fact checking. An orchestrator, specified as an explicit state machine
rather than as implicit control flow, coordinates the four.

\textbf{Second, the Verifier-to-Planner feedback loop, isolated by ablation.}
The loop is the difference between this system and the retrieve-once pipeline it
is measured against, so it is evaluated as a treatment rather than described as
a feature. An \texttt{agentic\_no\_verifier} configuration disables the backward
edge while holding everything else fixed, and the trace records which cycle
produced the answer that was ultimately selected, so the question ``did
re-planning actually help?'' is answerable from the logs rather than inferred
from an aggregate.

\textbf{Third, an evaluation protocol that prices agency.} Answer quality and
retrieval quality are reported alongside agent-specific cost --- model calls per
question, calls avoided by deterministic rules, tool invocations, plan depth,
re-plan rate, and latency --- so that no accuracy gain is presented without the
compute it consumed. The evaluation grid comprises nine configurations ---
lexical, dense, hybrid, naive-RAG and self-ask baselines, the agentic system
and its three ablations --- over two multi-hop benchmarks. At the time of
writing all nine have been run on HotpotQA, one of them still on a partial
slice, and six on 2WikiMultihopQA; Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} states
precisely which rows are complete, and no conclusion is drawn from a partial
one.

\textbf{Fourth, a fully local and reproducible implementation.} Every model runs
on-device, prompts are versioned files whose rendered SHA-1 is logged with each
call, every sort carries an explicit tiebreaker, and a test asserts that no
module imports a network client other than the local Ollama endpoint. The
system can be re-run by a reader with a consumer GPU and no budget.

Table~\ref{tab:task-mapping} records where each of the brief's five tasks is
implemented and where this report treats it, so that the coverage claim above
can be checked line by line rather than taken from the prose. One clause of the
brief is deliberately not met. Its description of the implementation chapter
mentions ``orchestration of external APIs for real-time data enrichment'', and
its Table~1 gives web search APIs as an example of a tool. This system exposes
no external API at all: every tool in the registry is a local index or the
local entity graph, and the network test above forbids anything else. The
deviation is a design decision rather than an omission. A tool whose output
changes between runs makes the evaluation unrepeatable and the trace
unfalsifiable, and the constraint of running with no paid service is one the
report treats as a feature of the study. The tool registry is written so that
a real-time connector could be added as one more entry, and
Section~\ref{sec:thesis} returns to that extension.

A fifth outcome is not a designed contribution but is reported because it is
useful. Schema-constrained planning with an 8B model proved more reliable than
expected on structure and less reliable than expected on self-description:
across the planning calls measured during development the model produced
correctly shaped decompositions in 8 of 8 cases with zero format retries, while
mislabelling its own plan's \texttt{strategy} field in 7 of those 8, including
obvious bridge and comparison cases. The system therefore derives the strategy
from the shape of the validated plan graph and overwrites whatever the model
claimed. Where a small model's self-report can and cannot be trusted is a
practical result for anyone building agents at this scale.

\section{Structure of This Document}

Chapter~\ref{ch:data} covers data and preprocessing. It explains why the
pretraining corpora listed in the brief's Table~2 cannot support an evaluation
requiring relevance judgements, substitutes two multi-hop retrieval benchmarks
that carry the necessary ground truth, and documents the passage segmentation,
deduplication, and stratified sampling that produce the evaluation and
calibration splits.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} surveys retrieval methodologies. It develops the
classical foundations --- sparse, dense, and fused retrieval with reranking ---
establishes why single-shot retrieval fails on compound needs, and then examines
the agentic alternatives corresponding to each of the brief's five tasks,
locating this system's design choices within that space.

Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} presents the implementation and the evaluation.
It specifies the orchestrator state machine, the per-agent contracts, the
indexing and budget-enforcement machinery, the baseline and ablation
configurations, and the metrics; it then reports results, ablations, and a
qualitative error analysis.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} assesses what the evidence supports, states the
limitations of the study without softening them, and sets out the work that
follows from it.
